What Fisher's discriminant does

Project every point onto a line with direction w. A good line pushes the two class averages far apart (large between-class gap) while keeping each class tightly bunched (small within-class spread). Fisher's score is their ratio:

J(w) = (wᵀ(μ₁ − μ₀))² / wᵀΣw

Maximizing J gives w ∝ Σ⁻¹(μ₁ − μ₀). No Gaussian assumption is made, and the answer is only a direction.

Things to try

Drag the angle slider and watch the two histograms separate or overlap. Set correlation to 0 with equal spreads: the best line is just the mean difference. Now raise the correlation: the best line rotates away from the mean difference, because Σ⁻¹ avoids directions where the noise is large.

What LDA adds

LDA is a probability model. It assumes each class is Gaussian with its own mean but one shared covariance Σ. Bayes' rule then gives a linear classifier:

P(class 1 | x) = 1 / (1 + e^−(wᵀx + b)), with w = Σ⁻¹(μ₁ − μ₀) and b = −½(μ₁ + μ₀)ᵀw + ln(π₁/π₀)

The decision boundary is the line wᵀx + b = 0, perpendicular to w. The ellipses show each class's 2σ contour: same shape, different centers.

Things to try

Change the class prior. The direction w stays put, but the boundary slides parallel to itself, away from the more common class. Fisher's method has no notion of priors, so it cannot do this.

The punchline: Fisher's direction and LDA's w are the same vector. Fisher derives it by maximizing separation. LDA derives it from Gaussian assumptions and also supplies the threshold b, including the effect of class priors. When the shared-covariance Gaussian assumption holds, LDA is the Bayes-optimal linear classifier.
